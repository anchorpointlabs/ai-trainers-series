# Training other trainers to teach AI

**Framework home** — Training and Adult Education (TAE) Skills Framework: *Learning Experience Delivery* (TAE-LDD-3008), *Technology-Enhanced Learning Delivery* (TAE-LDD-3009), *Learner Profile Analysis* (TAE-LDD-3011), *Assessment Design and Implementation* (TAE-LDD-3002). The credential behind it: WSQ ACLP (now ACLP 3.0). The ICT-side content standard: the nine GenAI TSCs.

---

## What it actually is

Teaching AI to trainers is not teaching AI. It is teaching people whose professional identity rests on **being the one who knows** to work in a domain where the content expires quarterly, the demo can fail live, the learners arrive with wildly unequal mental models, and the correct answer to a learner's question is sometimes "it depends on the model version".

Four things break when experienced trainers first teach AI:

1. **They teach features, not judgement.** Tool tours feel safe and go stale within a release cycle. Judgement — when to trust output, when to verify, when to escalate — survives model changes.
2. **They demo without a failure.** A demo where everything works teaches nothing about reliability. The pedagogical value is in the wobble: the hallucinated citation, the truncated answer, the prompt that worked yesterday.
3. **They assess knowledge, not capability.** Multiple-choice questions about "what is a token" are easy to mark and prove little. The TSC demands abilities — A3 anonymisation, A6 best practices, A8 comparing designs — which need practical assessment with criteria, not answer keys.
4. **They avoid the ethics conversation** because it feels like a trap. It is not a trap; it is a structured conversation with a framework behind it (TSC #8 and #9, plus IMDA's governance frameworks).

The trainer-of-trainers therefore works on three layers at once: the **content** (nine TSCs, K&As, levels), the **craft** (adult learning design, assessment, classroom management under uncertainty), and the **credibility** (being visibly current, and visibly honest about what you do not know).

## Why it matters at work

Singapore is pushing 100,000 workers toward "AI Bilingual" fluency under the National AI Impact Programme. That requires thousands of trainers who were taught to teach accounting, HR or Excel and are now asked to teach prompting. Most have no framework vocabulary, no failure-ready demos, and no idea that Basic and Intermediate are different TSCs. The bottleneck in AI training is not demand. It is trainers who can teach it properly.

## The student lens

(This entry is for the trainer-as-learner.)

**Mental model to install:** you are not the source of truth; you are the curator of a moving standard. Your job is to make the framework usable in a room, and to make your own obsolescence visible and planned.

**Misconceptions you will hear from experienced trainers:**
- *"I've been training for 20 years, this is just another topic."* The craft transfers. The content governance does not — you have never taught a subject with a quarterly-updated national skills taxonomy.
- *"If I show too many failures, they'll lose confidence in AI."* They will lose confidence in *you* if the demo fails and you have no explanation ready.
- *"Ethics is a 20-minute slot at the end."* It is a thread through every exercise; a learner who has not pasted something private and winced has not learned the privacy lesson.

## The adult educator lens

**Analogy:** teaching AI is like teaching someone to drive in a city that is rebuilding its roads every quarter. The vehicle changes, the rules change, the map is stale. What you can teach permanently is how to read signs, how to notice you are lost, and when to stop the car. That is judgement — and it is the only durable curriculum.

**Three-minute demo for trainers:** run the same prompt twice in front of them and let the outputs differ. Then ask: "what would you have said to a class if this happened to you at 10:15am?" Collect the answers, then give them the three-move recovery: name it ("models are non-deterministic, this is the same prompt"), explain it (one sentence, tokens and sampling), reframe it (here is why that is useful — you get options, not oracles).

**Board sketch:** three horizontal layers — CONTENT (9 TSCs, K/As, levels), CRAFT (design, assessment, classroom), CREDIBILITY (currency, honesty, evidence). Draw an arrow through all three labelled "what a trainer-of-trainers actually sells".

**Where it goes wrong in class:** a participant with 20 years' experience challenges the framework as bureaucratic. Do not defend the bureaucracy; show the audit trail. One Assessment Plan page with K5 mapped to a practical assessment ends the argument faster than any principle.

**Check for understanding:** "Your host centre hands you a deck with no K/A mapping and an assessment plan from two versions ago. What are the three questions you ask before you agree to run it?"
