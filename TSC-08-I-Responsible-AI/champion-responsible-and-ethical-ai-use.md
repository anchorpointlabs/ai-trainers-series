# Champion responsible and ethical AI use

> **TSC #8 Responsible AI and Generative AI Practices · Intermediate · Ability A9**

> **Framework text (verbatim):** "Champion best practices for responsible and ethical use of generative AI, addressing concerns about transparency, bias, and societal impact."
>
> **TSC #8** Responsible AI and Generative AI Practices · **Intermediate** · Ability · Bullet 14 of 14

**One-line version:** A champion is not the person who blocks the launch — it is the person who makes the safe launch the easy one, and who says the awkward thing early enough that it is still cheap.

## What it is

Championing responsible AI use means influencing practice from wherever you sit: raising a concern before it is expensive, attaching a fix to it, building the sanctioned path so people do not have to choose between speed and safety, and modelling the behaviour you are asking for. It is leadership without authority, and it usually fails on timing and framing rather than on the strength of the argument.

## Deep expansion

### The concept

Most responsible-AI failures are social before they are technical. Somebody saw the problem, and did not say it, because saying it would have delayed a demo in front of an executive. The champion is the person who changes that calculation — not by being louder, but by arriving with a specific risk, a cheap fix and a moment where the decision can still be changed.

That leads to the three-part structure of an effective challenge: **concern, evidence, fix**. A concern without evidence is an opinion; evidence without a fix is a veto; a fix without timing is a post-mortem. Learners who get good at this find that the job becomes easier, because the second challenge is expected and the third is welcomed.

The second half is building rather than objecting. A ban on consumer AI tools without an approved alternative produces shadow AI within a week — the practice simply moves out of sight. The champion's most durable contribution is often unglamorous: a sanctioned tool with zero retention, a template for the capability note, a pre-approved evaluation suite, a 24-hour fast lane for low-risk internal use.

### The sub-concepts

**Frame in the decision-maker's currency.** Cost, delay, reputation, regulatory exposure, customer churn. "This is ethically problematic" moves few budgets; "this will produce a rejection pattern we cannot defend in a tribunal" moves most.

**Bring a fix, with a cost.** "Run the disaggregated test — it takes half a day, it is already scripted, and if it passes we ship on Thursday." A fix with a size is a decision; a fix without one is homework.

**Timing is the whole game.** Before the demo, before the announcement, before the contract. After the launch, the same sentence becomes an audit finding.

**Build the paved road.** The sanctioned tool, the template, the pre-approved test. Convenience is the strongest compliance mechanism available.

**Escalate deliberately, not emotionally.** Peer, then lead, then risk or legal, then the regulator-adjacent route. Each step needs the previous one documented, and documentation protects both the champion and the organisation.

**Model the behaviour.** Disclose your own AI use, show your verification step, name the limits of something you built. People copy what they see, not what they are told.

**Measure the effect.** Override rates, incident counts, adoption of the sanctioned tool, time-to-approval. A champion who cannot show effect is a person with opinions.

### How it connects to the rest of the framework

`TSC8-Intermediate-K1` is the substance you are championing; `TSC8-Intermediate-K4` is the artefact — an honest capability note is the most persuasive object a champion can carry. `TSC8-Intermediate-A8` is the institutional form of the same work, and `TSC8-Intermediate-K5` gives you standing: citing an obligation is stronger than citing a preference. The whole TSC culminates here because this is the bullet where knowledge becomes behaviour.

### What is current as of November 2026

The stakes have risen in a way that helps the champion's argument. Agentic systems act — the current generation (GPT-6 Astra, Sol, Luna, 6.1 Sol, 6.1 Terra, Cyber; Claude 5.5; Gemini 3.1 Pro; Gemini 4 Argon) can call tools, browse, and change records — so "what can it do without asking" is now a question a risk committee understands immediately. Shadow AI remains the main leak, and procurement has become the highest-leverage control point: one clause in a vendor contract covers every future use of that tool.

Singapore's environment is unusually favourable for this role. The IMDA frameworks give a shared vocabulary, AI Verify and Project Moonshot give cheap evidence, and sector regulators give the argument teeth without requiring the champion to win a philosophical debate.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Championing means policing."* Policing finds violations after the fact; championing changes the default before it.
- *"Raise it and let them decide."* Without a fix and a cost, the decision defaults to shipping.
- *"Someone more senior owns this."* Senior people are usually further from the failure and later to hear about it.
- *"Being the blocker is leadership."* Blocking is easy and temporary. Building the fast safe path is leadership.

## How a student should understand it

**Mental model:** the colleague who puts a handrail on the stairs instead of a sign saying "be careful". One changes what happens; the other changes what is written down.

**Things they can do after this bullet:**
1. Script a 60-second challenge with concern, evidence, fix, cost and ask.
2. Identify one paved-road improvement they can ship in a month.
3. Document a concern so that it protects the organisation and themselves.

**Misconceptions to kill:** that championing is compliance work, that a concern without a fix counts as action, and that only senior people can do it.

## How a trainer explains it

### Analogies that work in a Singapore classroom

**Platform staff pointing you to the queue.** At a crowded interchange, the effective staff member does not shout "don't push" — they open a second lane and stand where the confusion is. Where it breaks: platform staff have visible authority; a champion in a project team usually has none, which is why the fix must be cheap enough to be accepted without escalation.

**The colleague who books the room and brings the agenda.** Nothing about that person's title makes the meeting happen. The meeting happens because they removed every reason for it not to.

### Worked example with a real tool

```text
My manager wants to skip the bias review to hit a launch date. I am the data analyst,
not the manager.

Write me three versions of a 60-second challenge:
1. To my manager directly — concern, evidence, fix with a time cost, and a clear ask.
2. To the risk lead, if the manager says no.
3. To the room at the launch review, if both fail.

Each version must name the specific harm, avoid the words "ethical" and "responsible"
as the main argument, and end with a question the other person must answer.
```

What to point at afterwards: the model's first draft will moralise, and the constraint forces it into the decision-maker's currency — cost, defensibility, timing. Point at the closing question in each version: a question transfers the decision rather than winning the argument, which is the skill being taught.

### Lesson plan (Gagné's nine events)

1. **Gain attention** — tell a two-line story of a launch that went ahead and cost more than the delay would have.
2. **State the objective** — by the end you can script and deliver a challenge that changes a decision.
3. **Recall prior learning** — ask what happened the last time someone in the room raised a risk.
4. **Present the content** — concern/evidence/fix, framing, timing, paved road, escalation ladder.
5. **Guide** — draft the manager version together and cut every moralising word.
6. **Elicit performance** — pairs role-play the 60 seconds; the listener plays a deadline-pressured manager.
7. **Give feedback** — the listener says whether they would change their decision, and why not.
8. **Transfer** — each learner writes one paved-road improvement they will build this month.

## What the framework doesn't mention but trainers need to know

- **You need one ally with authority.** Champions who work alone are remembered as difficult; champions with a sponsor are remembered as early.
- **Document the concern.** A dated note protects the individual and gives the organisation a chance to act — and it is the single most useful artefact after an incident.
- **Pick two battles a quarter.** The person who raises everything is heard on nothing.
- **Celebrate the fix, not the objection.** Credit is how the behaviour spreads.

## Watch out

- **Do not teach moralising.** The room must leave with a script, not a conviction.
- **Do not let learners win the argument and lose the meeting.** Effectiveness is the measure, not correctness.
- **Do not promise that raising it will be welcomed.** Sometimes it is not, and the honest answer includes what to do then.
- **The stale claim to retire:** "responsible AI is the compliance team's job." In November 2026 it is a behaviour distributed across everyone who touches the system, and the champion is the person who makes it normal.
